===== HARDNESS INVESTIGATION =====
## Evidence map

### Direct Coffee Evidence
*   **Hendon, Colonna-Dashwood, & Colonna-Dashwood (2014)**: Density-functional theory (DFT) models and experimental extractions demonstrating that divalent cations ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{Ca}^{2+}$) extract oxygen-rich flavor compounds (chlorogenic acids, citric acid, malic acid, lactic acid, caffeine) significantly more efficiently than monovalent cations ($\text{Na}^+$) or pure water. $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ exhibits a higher binding energy to polar flavor molecules than $\text{Ca}^{2+}$.
*   **Navarini & Rivetti (2010)**: Review of aqueous extraction mechanics in espresso, documenting the effect of calcium/magnesium hardness and bicarbonate buffering on perceived acidity, crema stability, and lipid emulsification.
*   **Batali, Ristenpart, & Guinard (2020, 2022) / UC Davis Coffee Center**: Sensory descriptive analysis establishing that extraction yield (EY) and total dissolved solids (TDS) drive the primary sensory space, while specific ionic compositions modulate attribute intensity (acidity, bitterness, astringency, sweetness) within fixed TDS/EY windows.
*   **SCA Technical Standards (2018)**: Target ranges for total hardness ($50\text{--}175\text{ mg/L CaCO}_3$ equivalent) and alkalinity ($40\text{--}70\text{ mg/L CaCO}_3$ equivalent).

### General Chemistry Inference
*   **Coordination Chemistry & Hydration Enthalpy**: $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ has a smaller ionic radius ($\sim 72\text{ pm}$) than $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ($\sim 100\text{ pm}$), yielding a higher charge-to-size ratio and stronger hydration shell. This gives $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ higher thermodynamic binding affinity for oxygen-donor ligands (carboxylates, phenolic hydroxyls).
*   **Carbonate Equilibrium & Solubility**: $\text{CaCO}_3$ has a significantly lower solubility product constant ($K_{sp} \approx 3.3 \times 10^{-9}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$) than $\text{MgCO}_3$ ($K_{sp} \approx 6.8 \times 10^{-6}$). Heating accelerates $\text{CaCO}_3$ precipitation (limescale), removing $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and bicarbonate from solution while generating $CO_2$ gas.

### Coffee-Community Heuristic
*   **Colonna-Dashwood & Hendon (2015) / Barista Community Rules-of-Thumb**: The assertion that $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ selectively extracts "bright, fruity, acidic" notes, whereas $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ selectively extracts "heavy, sweet, creamy" notes.
*   **Fixed Ion Ratios**: The rule of thumb suggesting an optimal $3:1$ or $2:1$ Magnesium-to-Calcium ratio for manual pourover brewing.

---

## Mechanisms

### Thermodynamic Binding and Extraction Yield
Divalent cations act as thermodynamic extraction agents during brewing. Many volatile and non-volatile coffee flavor compounds contain polar, oxygen-rich functional groups (carboxylates, hydroxyls, carbonyls). $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ form coordinate complexes with these oxygen-donor sites in the solid coffee matrix, lowering the activation energy required to dissolve them into the aqueous phase.

*   **Magnesium ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$)**: Due to its smaller ionic radius, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ possesses a higher charge density than $\text{Ca}^{2+}$. DFT calculations (Hendon et al., 2014) confirm that $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ forms stronger binding complexes with oxygen-rich targets (e.g., chlorogenic acid, citric acid, malic acid) than $\text{Ca}^{2+}$. This facilitates higher kinetic mass transfer of light, highly polar organic acids during short brew times.
*   **Calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$)**: Possesses a lower charge density and larger ionic radius. While still substantially more effective at binding flavor compounds than $\text{Na}^+$ or pure $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, its binding energies for low-molecular-weight organic acids are lower than those of $\text{Mg}^{2+}$. However, $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ interacts more readily with high-molecular-weight compounds, including lipids, proteins, and larger polyphenols.

### Mass Transfer, Matrix Interaction, and Precipitation
*   **Pore Diffusion and Matrix Swelling**: Both ions increase ionic strength, which alters cellular swelling in the ground coffee matrix and impacts pore diffusion rates during percolation.
*   **Insoluble Salt Formation**: At elevated concentrations ($>100\text{ mg/L}$ as $\text{CaCO}_3$) and elevated temperatures ($>90^\circ\text{C}$), $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ reacts with dissolved bicarbonate ($\text{HCO}_3^-$) and oxalic/fatty acids, precipitating out of solution. This reduces available extraction energy and leaves insoluble micro-particulates that alter tactile perception. $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ remains soluble across standard brewing temperatures and alkalinity ranges.

## Sensory findings

### Acidity
*   **$\text{Mg}^{2+}$ Effect**: Increases perceived sharpness and brightness of acidity. By accelerating the extraction of low-molecular-weight aliphatic organic acids (citric, malic, quinic), high $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ content amplifies malic/citric acid perception, provided bicarbonate alkalinity is low enough not to buffer the resulting $\text{H}^+$ ions.
*   **$\text{Ca}^{2+}$ Effect**: Tends to produce a rounded, muted acidity. While $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ extracts organic acids, its affinity for high-molecular-weight matrix components and its propensity to co-precipitate with buffering anions lowers peak tartness.

### Sweetness
*   **Sensory Mechanism**: Sweetness in black coffee is largely an olfactory and tactile construct driven by sucrose hydrolysis products, furans, and balanced extraction of organic acids without overwhelming bitterness. Neither ion is intrinsically "sweet."
*   **$\text{Mg}^{2+}$ vs. $\text{Ca}^{2+}$**: Optimal $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ enhances perceived sweetness by maximizing fruit-like aromatic/acid complexity. Optimal $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ enhances perceived sweetness by adding tactile weight and softening harsh organic acids. Excessive levels of either ion reduce perceived sweetness through over-extraction of bitter polyphenols.

### Body and Mouthfeel
*   **$\text{Ca}^{2+}$ Dominance**: $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ significantly increases tactile body and perceived viscosity. It binds and stabilizes larger polyphenolic aggregates, melanoidins, and emulsified lipids, enhancing crema volume/persistence in espresso and mouthfeel weight in drip filter coffee.
*   **$\text{Mg}^{2+}$ Impact**: Yields a lean, clean, high-clarity tactile profile with minimal heavy viscosity.

### Clarity vs. Astringency
*   **Clarity**: High $\text{Mg}^{2+}/\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ratios favor flavor separation and distinct articulate acidity, provided total ionic strength is kept moderate ($50\text{--}100\text{ mg/L}$ total hardness).
*   **Astringency**: Both ions increase astringency (puckering sensation) when overconcentrated ($>150\text{ mg/L}$ total hardness). Divalent cations cross-link salivary proline-rich proteins, accelerating their precipitation when polyphenols (chlorogenic acid lactones, dicaffeoylquinic acids) are extracted at high yields.

### Concentration Dependence
*   **Low Concentration ($<30\text{ mg/L}$ total hardness)**: Under-extraction of key flavor compounds; beverage tastes flat, hollow, sharp, and sour (if alkalinity is low) or papery.
*   **Optimal Concentration ($50\text{--}125\text{ mg/L}$ total hardness)**: Balanced extraction of sugars, organic acids, and aromatics with pleasing mouthfeel.
*   **High Concentration ($>150\text{ mg/L}$ total hardness)**: Excessive extraction of heavy bitter compounds; dry, chalky, astringent finish; metallic off-flavors (especially from high $\text{Mg}^{2+}$).

### Roast Level Dependence
*   **Light Roasts**: Possess high concentrations of intact organic acids (citric, malic) and dense cell structures requiring higher kinetic extraction energy. Light roasts benefit from higher $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ levels ($2:1$ to $3:1$ $\text{Mg}:\text{Ca}$) to drive tart acid extraction and fruity volatile recovery.
*   **Dark Roasts**: Highly degraded cellular matrix, lower organic acid content, higher proportion of bitter chlorogenic acid lactones and phenylindanes. High $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ or $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ easily over-extracts harsh, burnt bitterness. Dark roasts benefit from lower total hardness ($30\text{--}60\text{ mg/L}$) and lower $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ levels to preserve sweetness and limit astringency.

## Interactions

### Hardness vs. Alkalinity Interactions
The sensory impact of $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ cannot be isolated from total alkalinity (bicarbonate, $\text{HCO}_3^-$):

*   **High Hardness + Low Alkalinity**: Yields an unbuffered, highly acidic, potentially sour and aggressive extraction. The divalent cations drive high extraction yield of organic acids, while low alkalinity fails to neutralize free $\text{H}^+$ ions.
*   **High Hardness + High Alkalinity**: High extraction yield coupled with heavy acid buffering. Organic acids are neutralized into their conjugate salts (e.g., sodium/calcium citrate), causing a dull, chalky, flat beverage with high bitterness and lingering astringency.
*   **Low Hardness + High Alkalinity**: Under-extracted flavor compounds combined with severe acid neutralization; tastes soapy, flat, and hollow.

### Sulfate ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$) and Chloride ($\text{Cl}^-$) Counter-Ion Confounding
The anions paired with $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ strongly alter flavor structure:
*   **Sulfate ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$)**: Accentuates dry, crisp, focused acidity and highlights hop-like or roasty bitterness. High $\text{MgSO}_4$ accentuates brightness but risks a dry, astringent finish.
*   **Chloride ($\text{Cl}^-$)**: Accentuates sweetness, mouthfeel, and texture. High $\text{CaCl}_2$ amplifies body and smoothness, but excessive levels ($>200\text{ mg/L}$) impart a salty, damp, or medicinal taste and accelerate equipment corrosion.

### Over-Target Caveats
1.  **Limescale Risk**: Excess $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ($>80\text{ mg/L}$) combined with alkalinity $>50\text{ mg/L}$ causes rapid $\text{CaCO}_3$ precipitation in boilers, group heads, and kettles, deteriorating heat transfer and blocking valves.
2.  **Metallic/Chalky Off-Flavors**: Elevated $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ($>100\text{ mg/L}$) imparts a distinct metallic, bitter, or salty flavor. Elevated $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ($>100\text{ mg/L}$) creates a chalky, heavy mouthfeel that obscures volatile origin characteristics.

---

## What not to claim

1.  **Do not claim universal taste thresholds**: Avoid asserting that specific PPM values (e.g., "70 mg/L Mg") guarantee a specific sensory result across all coffees, roast profiles, and brew ratios.
2.  **Do not claim calcium directly neutralizes acidity**: Calcium cations do not neutralize acid; bicarbonate anions ($\text{HCO}_3^-$) perform chemical buffering. Calcium dampens perceived acidity via sensory masking, matrix extraction dynamics, and lipid binding.
3.  **Do not claim magnesium intrinsically tastes sweet**: Magnesium drives extraction efficiency of polar compounds; it does not possess an intrinsically sweet flavor.
4.  **Do not claim fixed cation ratios are universally ideal**: Ratios like $3:1$ $\text{Mg}:\text{Ca}$ are taste heuristics, not fundamental laws of chemistry. Optimal ratios depend on roast depth, bean origin, and anion balance ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}/\text{Cl}^-$).

===== BUFFERING INVESTIGATION =====
### Evidence map

#### Direct coffee evidence
* **Hendon, Colonna-Dashwood, & Colonna-Dashwood (2014)** (*J. Agric. Food Chem.*): Explores ionic extraction in coffee. Demonstrates that while cations ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{Ca}^{2+}$, $\text{Na}^+$) drive extractability of flavor compounds, the bicarbonate anion ($\text{HCO}_3^-$) acts strictly as a thermodynamic acid buffer, regulating the protonation state of extracted organic acids.
* **Navarini & Rivetti (2010)** (*Physico-Chemical Aspects of Coffee Brew*, *Food Chemistry*): Outlines acid-base equilibria in coffee brew. Shows that intrinsic organic acids (chlorogenic, citric, malic, acetic, quinic) dictate final brew pH, and incoming water buffer capacity determines the degree of acid deprotonation.
* **Batali, Ristenpart, & Cotter (2020, 2021)** (*UC Davis Coffee Center*, *J. Food Science*): Evaluates titratable acidity, TDS, and sensory profiles. Proves sensory acidity correlates more strongly with titratable acidity and brew pH than total dissolved solids alone.
* **SCA / WCR Water Quality Standards (2009, 2018)**: Sets target alkalinity at $\sim 40 \text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$ ($\approx 48.8 \text{ mg/L HCO}_3^-$), establishing community standards for balancing acidity without causing corrosion or heavy scale.

#### General chemistry inference
* **Stumm & Morgan (1996)** (*Aquatic Chemistry*): Establishes inorganic carbon speciation ($\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3^*$, $\text{HCO}_3^-$, $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$). In typical brewing water ($\text{pH } 6.0\text{--}8.5$), $\text{HCO}_3^-$ is the dominant species ($>95\%$). $pK_{a1} \approx 6.35$ and $pK_{a2} \approx 10.33$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$.
* **Snoeyink & Jenkins (1980)** (*Water Chemistry*): Governs carbonate equilibria and scale formation kinetics ($\text{Ca}^{2+} + 2\text{HCO}_3^- \rightleftharpoons \text{CaCO}_3\downarrow + \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$).

#### Coffee-community heuristic
* Bicarbonate target ranges between $30\text{--}50 \text{ ppm}$ ($\text{mg/L HCO}_3^-$) are widely recommended for filter coffee to preserve origin character.
* Rule of thumb: "High bicarbonate ruins light roasts; zero bicarbonate makes dark roasts undrinkable." (Heuristic based on acid-to-buffer ratios).

---

### Mechanisms

#### Chemical distinctions
* **Bicarbonate ($\text{HCO}_3^-$):** The specific monovalent anion responsible for acid buffering in typical drinking water ($\text{pH } 6.0\text{--}8.5$).
* **Alkalinity:** A aggregate quantitative measure of water's capacity to neutralize acids down to a specific pH endpoint ($\sim 4.5$). While bicarbonate usually accounts for $>98\%$ of total alkalinity in standard potable water, alkalinity can also include hydroxide ($\text{OH}^-$), carbonate ($\text{CO}_3^{2-}$), phosphate ($\text{HPO}_4^{2-}$), and silicate species.
* **Carbonate ($\text{CO}_3^{2-}$):** The divalent anion formed when $pH > 8.3$. It is virtually absent in neutral brewing water, making claims about "carbonate buffer" chemically inaccurate for brewing water unless the water is extremely alkaline.
* **Final Brew pH:** The thermodynamic hydrogen ion concentration ($-\log[\text{H}^+]$) of the prepared beverage. Driven primarily by coffee organic acid yield (which can drop brew pH to $4.8\text{--}5.2$) reacting against the water's initial buffer capacity.

#### Acid neutralization pathway
$$\text{HCO}_3^- + \text{H}^+ \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3 \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2(\text{aq})$$
When coffee organic acids ($\text{HA}$) dissolve, they release protons ($\text{H}^+$). Bicarbonate consumes these protons, shifting the organic acids into their conjugated base forms ($\text{A}^-$). Conjugated bases (e.g., citrate, malate) have significantly lower perceived sourness than their protonated acid counterparts ($\text{HA}$).

#### Extraction dynamics
Bicarbonate does not significantly increase or decrease mass extraction yield (TDS) of non-ionic target compounds. However, by altering local pH during extraction, it modifies the ionization state of polyphenols and organic acids, altering their solubility rates and volatile release.

---

### Sensory findings

#### Impact on perceived acidity
* **Deprotonation effect:** High $\text{HCO}_3^-$ neutralizes free $\text{H}^+$ ions, lowering titratable acidity in the cup and raising brew pH. This directly attenuates perceived brightness, tartness, and fruit-like acidity.
* **Under-buffered water ($\text{HCO}_3^- < 15\text{ mg/L}$):** Fails to buffer harsh, low-pH organic acids. Results in sharp, vinegar-like, aggressive, or sour-dominant sensory profiles.
* **Over-buffered water ($\text{HCO}_3^- > 80\text{ mg/L}$):** Excessively neutralizes acids. Results in flat, dull, chalky, or flabby cups, often amplifying perceived bitterness and astringency.

#### Roast dependence
* **Light Roasts:** Retain high levels of chlorogenic, citric, and malic acids. They tolerate moderate bicarbonate levels ($\sim 30\text{--}50\text{ mg/L}$) to temper intense sharp notes, but high bicarbonate strips their distinctive floral and fruit notes.
* **Dark Roasts:** Organic acids are degraded during pyrolysis; intrinsic acid levels are low. High bicarbonate strips remaining structure, making dark roasts taste overly bitter, dry, ashy, and soapy. Dark roasts benefit from lower bicarbonate ($\sim 15\text{--}30\text{ mg/L}$).

---

### Interactions

#### Calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$) and Magnesium ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$) interactions
* **Thermal precipitation (Scaling):** Upon heating, bicarbonate decomposes, shifting equilibrium to carbonate and precipitating calcium carbonate:
  $$\text{Ca}^{2+} + 2\text{HCO}_3^- \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{CaCO}_3(\text{s}) \downarrow + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2(\text{g})$$
* Elevated $\text{HCO}_3^-$ combined with high $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ causes heavy limescale accumulation in heating elements and thermoblocks.
* $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ scales far less readily than $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ at standard steam boiler temperatures; thus, high bicarbonate combined with pure magnesium water carries a lower scaling risk than with calcium water.

#### Dose and Context Dependence
* **Brew Ratio (Espresso vs. Filter):** Espresso uses a high coffee-to-water ratio ($\sim 1:2$). The massive mass of coffee grounds contributes an overwhelming load of organic acids relative to the small volume of water, rendering espresso somewhat less sensitive to incoming water buffer than drip filter coffee ($\sim 1:16$), where water buffer volume is proportionally $8\times$ higher relative to coffee mass.

---

### What not to claim

* **Do NOT claim a single universal ppm threshold for taste.** Sensory thresholds depend on bean origin, roast profile, extraction ratio, and individual human sensitivity.
* **Do NOT claim bicarbonate directly extracts coffee solids.** Extraction yield of sugars, lipids, and aromatics is driven by water temperature, contact time, particle size, and cation binding ($\text{Mg}^{2+}/\text{Ca}^{2+}$), not by the bicarbonate anion.
* **Do NOT equate bicarbonate strictly with carbonate or total hardness.** Hardness measures divalent cations ($\text{Ca}^{2+}, \text{Mg}^{2+}$); bicarbonate is a monovalent buffer anion.
* **Do NOT claim high bicarbonate lowers extraction yield.** It alters sensory balance via acid neutralization, not by stopping physical dissolution.

---

### Candidate tooltip

Bicarbonate ($\text{HCO}_3^-$) buffers coffee acids. Balanced levels ($30\text{--}50\text{ mg/L}$) smooth out harshness while preserving bright, fruit notes. Excessive bicarbonate mutes flavor and causes boiler scale with calcium; too little produces overly sharp, sour brews.

### Sodium ($\text{Na}^+$)

#### 1. Extraction Effects
Hendon et al. (2014) demonstrated that monovalent ions like $\text{Na}^+$ exhibit lower binding affinity for organic flavor compounds (such as chlorogenic acid species, caffeic acid, and citric acid) than divalent cations ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{Ca}^{2+}$). Sodium ions do not readily form stable coordination complexes with oxygen-rich organic species in the coffee bed. Consequently, varying $\text{Na}^+$ in brew water within potable limits ($0\text{--}100\text{ mg/L}$) yields negligible changes in overall Extraction Yield (EY) or total dissolved solids (TDS) compared to changes in divalent cation concentrations.

#### 2. Sensory Perception & Taste Interactions
The primary sensory mechanism of $\text{Na}^+$ in coffee is **trans-modal bitterness suppression** rather than direct salt taste perception. Breslin & Beauchamp (1995, 1997) proved that sodium salts selectively suppress bitter taste responses at the receptor level while releasing sweet and savory flavors from background suppression. 
* **Detection Thresholds:** Absolute saltiness thresholds for $\text{NaCl}$ in pure water range from $100\text{ mg/L}$ to $300\text{ mg/L}$ ($1.7\text{--}5.1\text{ mM}$). In coffee, high background matrix intensity (caffeine, chlorogenic acid lactones, phenylindanes) elevates the detection threshold for a purely "salty" off-note.
* **Low-Dose Modulation ($10\text{--}30\text{ mg/L}$):** Suppresses sharp bitterness without contributing a salty taste, indirectly enhancing perceived sweetness and roundness.

#### 3. Roast & Context Dependence
* **Dark Roasts:** Higher concentration of bitter phenylindanes and lactones makes dark roasts more responsive to bitterness suppression via low-level $\text{Na}^+$ addition.
* **Light Roasts:** Excessive sodium ($>50\text{ mg/L}$) can mute desirable delicate organic acidity, flattening the origin characteristics.

---

### Potassium ($\text{K}^+$)

#### 1. Natural Coffee Matrix Dominance
Potassium is the most abundant inorganic cation in green and roasted coffee beans, comprising roughly $1.5\text{--}2.0\%$ of dry bean mass (Clarke & Vitzthum, 2001; Speer & Kölling-Speer, 2006). During extraction, $\text{K}^+$ readily dissolves, yielding $200\text{--}1000\text{ mg/L}$ of $\text{K}^+$ in liquid coffee. 
* **Brew Water Impact:** Water containing $5\text{--}10\text{ mg/L}$ of $\text{K}^+$ contributes less than $2\text{--}5\%$ of the total potassium present in the final cup. Thus, adjusting input water potassium has an insignificant effect on extraction dynamics or overall cation balance in the beverage.

#### 2. Sensory Perception
* **Off-Note Profile:** Unlike $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{K}^+$ triggers both salty and bitter taste receptors simultaneously (Lawless et al., 2003). At concentrations above $50\text{--}100\text{ mg/L}$ in water, potassium salts ($\text{KCl}$, $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_4$) elicit distinct metallic, astringent, and harsh bitter tastes.
* **Role in Water Solutions:** Adding $\text{K}^+$ salts to brew water offers no sensory advantage over $\text{Na}^+$ salts and carries a significantly higher risk of introducing metallic off-notes.

---

### Chloride ($\text{Cl}^-$)

#### 1. Sensory Effects
Chloride acts as a passive background anion in taste perception but influences solution flavor dynamics:
* **Mouthfeel & Sweetness:** In general aqueous sensory literature, $\text{Cl}^-$ ions combined with sodium or calcium enhance perceived body, viscosity, and sweetness perception.
* **Off-Notes:** When $\text{Cl}^-$ exceeds $100\text{--}150\text{ mg/L}$ (especially when paired with high $\text{Na}^+$), it produces a sharp, brackish, or medicinal taste.

#### 2. Machine Corrosion & Engineering Ceilings
The primary constraint on chloride in coffee water is **corrosion chemistry**, not sensory preference.
* **Pitting Corrosion:** $\text{Cl}^-$ ions break down the passive chromium oxide ($\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_3$) protective layer on stainless steel boilers and heat exchangers. Under heat ($>90^\circ\text{C}$) and pressure, $\text{Cl}^-$ initiates localized pitting and stress corrosion cracking (SCC).
* **Engineering Limits:** Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) water specifications set target chloride at $0\text{--}30\text{ mg/L}$. Boiler manufacturers (e.g., La Marzocco) explicitly void warranties if water chloride levels exceed $30\text{--}50\text{ mg/L}$, regardless of total hardness or alkalinity.

---

### Sulfate ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$)

#### 1. General Chemistry & Sensory Perception
Sulfate ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$) is a divalent anion commonly introduced via Epsom salt ($\text{MgSO}_4\cdot 7\text{H}_2\text{O}$) or gypsum ($\text{CaSO}_4\cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$).
* **Sensory Effects:** In drinking water quality standards (WHO), high concentrations of sulfate ($>250\text{ mg/L}$) introduce a noticeable dry, chalky, or astringent taste and can act as a laxative ($>500\text{ mg/L}$). At moderate levels ($50\text{--}150\text{ mg/L}$), it contributes a crisp, mineral quality.

#### 2. The $\text{Cl}^-:\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ Ratio Myth in Coffee
The hypothesis that a specific **Chloride-to-Sulfate ratio** controls the balance between "sweet/full" and "bright/acidic" flavors is imported directly from **beer brewing chemistry** (Palmer & Kaminski, *Water: A Comprehensive Guide for Brewers*). 
* **Brewing Application:** In beer, $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ enhances the crispness and bitterness perception of hop alpha-acids, while $\text{Cl}^-$ enhances maltiness and fullness.
* **Coffee Context:** **There are no peer-reviewed studies validating the $\text{Cl}^-:\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ ratio as a primary driver of flavor balance in coffee extraction.** Coffee's structural bitterness and acidity stem from chlorogenic acid breakdown products, aliphatic acids, and caffeine, which operate differently from hop iso-alpha-acids and malt dextrins.

---

### Pairing & Confounding

#### 1. Counter-Ion Confounding
Ions cannot be added in isolation; they must be introduced as neutral salts. Disentangling the effect of a cation from its counter-anion requires controlled factorial experimental design:

$$\text{MgSO}_4 \longrightarrow \text{Mg}^{2+} + \text{SO}_4^{2-}$$

$$\text{MgCl}_2 \longrightarrow \text{Mg}^{2+} + 2\text{Cl}^-$$

* **Confounding Example:** Testing $\text{MgSO}_4$ against $\text{CaCl}_2$ confounds two variables at once: the cation ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$ vs. $\text{Ca}^{2+}$) and the anion ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ vs. $\text{Cl}^-$). Observed differences in acidity or body cannot be attributed solely to the cation without testing $\text{MgCl}_2$ and $\text{CaSO}_4$ controls.

#### 2. Ionic Strength ($\mu$) Calculation
Ionic strength measures the total concentration of electric charge in solution:

$$\mu = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i} c_i z_i^2$$

Where $c_i$ is molar concentration and $z_i$ is charge number.

* **Impact on Extraction Kinetics:** Divalent ions ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{Ca}^{2+}$) contribute $4\times$ more to ionic strength than monovalent ions ($\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{K}^+$) per mole. Higher ionic strength reduces activity coefficients ($\gamma_i$) of polar organic species according to the Extended Debye-Hückel / Davies equations, slightly altering organic compound solubility during extraction independent of specific ionic binding.

---

### What Not to Claim

1. **Do not claim universal taste detection thresholds for individual ions.** Taste sensitivity varies by individual, and the complex organic matrix of coffee suppresses salty/bitter perception compared to pure water.
2. **Do not state that the Chloride-to-Sulfate ratio is a proven coffee tuning mechanism.** Frame it strictly as an unvalidated heuristic borrowed from brewing science.
3. **Do not attribute primary extraction power to Sodium or Potassium.** Extraction yields of organic acids and volatile compounds are driven predominantly by temperature, grind size, contact time, and divalent cations ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{Ca}^{2+}$).
4. **Do not confuse chloride flavor preferences with equipment safety.** High chloride limits are strictly bound by stainless steel corrosion mechanics ($<30\text{ mg/L}$).

---

### Candidate Tooltips

#### Sodium ($\text{Na}^+$)
> Suppresses bitter notes and highlights background sweetness at low levels (~10–30 mg/L). Excessive sodium mutes delicate origin acidity or causes salty off-flavors. Monovalent sodium has negligible impact on compound extraction efficiency compared to calcium or magnesium.

#### Potassium ($\text{K}^+$)
> Coffee beans naturally release massive amounts of potassium during brewing (~200–1000 mg/L). Water additions contribute negligible extra potassium and risk introducing a metallic, bitter, or harsh taste at lower thresholds than sodium.

#### Chloride ($\text{Cl}^-$)
> Enhances perceived body and sweetness in low doses. Keep below 30 mg/L to prevent irreversible pitting corrosion in espresso machine boilers. High levels yield sharp, brackish, or medicinal off-tastes.

#### Sulfate ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$)
> Increases water ionic strength, adding a crisp or dry mineral quality. Excess concentrations cause chalky, bitter off-tastes. Flavor claims regarding specific chloride-to-sulfate ratios are borrowed from beer brewing and lack peer-reviewed coffee proof.

===== CITRATE INVESTIGATION =====
### Evidence Map

* **Direct Coffee Evidence:**
  * Native citric acid content in green and roasted coffee, its contribution to beverage titratable acidity, and sensory sourness (*Flament, 2002*; *Yeager et al., 2021, UC Davis Coffee Center*).
  * Extraction dynamics of divalent cations ($\text{Ca}^{2+}, \text{Mg}^{2+}$) binding to coffee flavor compounds (*Hendon et al., 2014*).
  * *Gap:* Peer-reviewed sensory panels explicitly isolating *added water-borne citrate ions* versus inorganic bicarbonate buffers in controlled coffee extractions remain extremely sparse.

* **General Chemistry Inference:**
  * Triprotic acid ionization constants ($pK_a$ values) and aqueous speciation of citric acid/citrate species across typical water and brew pH ranges ($pH\ 3.0\text{--}7.0$) (*NIST Critically Selected Stability Constants*).
  * Chelation thermodynamics and formation constants of soluble complexes ($\text{CaCit}^-$, $\text{MgCit}^-$) suppressing free metal ion activity (*Martell & Smith, 1989*).
  * Non-volatile organic buffer capacity calculations (Van Slyke equations) compared to the volatile carbonic acid/bicarbonate system ($\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3/\text{HCO}_3^-$).

* **Coffee-Community Heuristic:**
  * Usage of trisodium citrate ($\text{Na}_3\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{O}_7$) or potassium citrate in remineralized brewing water (e.g., Barista Hustle recipes, commercial re-mineralization drops) to provide a "soft" acid buffer alternative to bicarbonate without adding carbonate hardness.

---

### Chemistry

Citric acid ($\text{H}_3\text{Cit}$) is a triprotic organic acid with temperature-dependent dissociation constants at standard conditions ($25^\circ\text{C}$):
* $pK_{a1} \approx 3.13 \quad (\text{H}_3\text{Cit} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{Cit}^- + \text{H}^+)$
* $pK_{a2} \approx 4.76 \quad (\text{H}_2\text{Cit}^- \rightleftharpoons \text{HCit}^{2-} + \text{H}^+)$
* $pK_{a3} \approx 6.40 \quad (\text{HCit}^{2-} \rightleftharpoons \text{Cit}^{3-} + \text{H}^+)$

In standard brew water ($pH\ 6.5\text{--}7.5$), citrate exists predominantly as hydrogen citrate ($\text{HCit}^{2-}$) and fully deprotonated citrate ($\text{Cit}^{3-}$). In the final beverage ($pH\ 4.8\text{--}5.5$), the distribution shifts heavily toward bicitrate ($\text{H}_2\text{Cit}^-$) and hydrogen citrate ($\text{HCit}^{2-}$).

```
   100% |   H3Cit    H2Cit⁻     HCit²⁻      Cit³⁻
        |    /\       /\         /\          /\
Percent |   /  \     /  \       /  \        /  \
Species |  /    \   /    \     /    \      /    \
     0% +---+----+---+----+---+----+---+----+---+--> pH
            3.13     4.76      6.40
```

#### Chelation Dynamics
Citrate acts as a multidentate chelating ligand. It binds divalent cations ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$) to form stable, soluble complexes:
$$\text{Ca}^{2+} + \text{Cit}^{3-} \rightleftharpoons \text{CaCit}^- \quad (\log K \approx 3.15\text{--}4.8)$$
$$\text{Mg}^{2+} + \text{Cit}^{3-} \rightleftharpoons \text{MgCit}^- \quad (\log K \approx 3.2\text{--}3.4)$$

This complexation significantly reduces the thermodynamic activity of free $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ions ($\gamma_M \cdot [M^{2+}]$) in the aqueous phase without removing the minerals from total dissolved solids (TDS).

---

### Coffee Relevance

#### Added Water Citrate vs. Native Coffee Acids
* **Native Organic Acids:** Coffee beans naturally contain approximately $0.5\text{--}2.5\%$ dry weight citric acid (along with malic, quinic, and chlorogenic acids). During extraction, these organic acids dissolve into the brew, creating the coffee's intrinsic titratable acidity and lowering final brew pH to $\sim 4.8\text{--}5.3$.
* **Added Water Citrate:** Introducing citrate species ($\text{Na}_3\text{Cit}$, $\text{K}_3\text{Cit}$, or citric acid) via the water source changes the baseline chemical environment *before* contact with coffee grounds. 

#### Extraction Dynamics
Inorganic calcium and magnesium extract oxygen-rich polar compounds (flavonoids, chlorogenic acids) via ion-dipole and coordination interactions (*Hendon et al., 2014*). When citrate is present in brewing water, it competes with these coffee compounds by chelating free $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$. This binding can decrease the effective extraction efficiency of certain high-molecular-weight polyphenols.

#### Non-Volatile Buffering
Unlike bicarbonate ($\text{HCO}_3^-$), which reacts with protons to form carbonic acid ($\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$) and degassing carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$), citrate remains in solution as a non-volatile buffer system centered around $pK_{a2}\ (4.76)$ and $pK_{a3}\ (6.40)$. Added citrate salts act as an organic buffer directly in the pH zone of brewed coffee.

---

### Interactions

* **Calcium and Magnesium:** Chelation keeps calcium and magnesium in soluble complex forms ($\text{CaCit}^-$, $\text{MgCit}^-$). This suppresses the precipitation of calcium carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$) scale in heating elements and boilers.
* **Alkalinity Metrics:** Standard titration measuring total alkalinity to a methyl orange end-point ($pH \approx 4.3\text{--}4.5$) counts citrate species ($\text{Cit}^{3-}$, $\text{HCit}^{2-}$, and partially $\text{H}_2\text{Cit}^-$) as alkalinity because they accept protons during titration. However, its buffering curve differs distinctly from bicarbonate.
* **Perceived Taste:**
  * *Low concentrations:* Enhances perceived brightness or provides a smooth, organic buffer curve.
  * *High concentrations:* Counterions ($\text{Na}^+$ or $\text{K}^+$) along with excess organic acid anions can yield an unnatural salty-sour taste or overly mute intrinsic origin acidity.

---

### Safety and Uncertainty Boundaries

* **No Universal Taste Threshold:** Universal sensory thresholds cannot be stated as a single numeric value. Perception varies based on background mineral composition, roast degree, extraction yield, and individual human sensitivity.
* **Lack of Peer-Reviewed Extraction Studies:** While the aqueous chemistry of citrate-metal complexation is established, direct scientific literature measuring compound-by-compound extraction yields using citrate-remineralized water vs. standard inorganic water remains sparse.
* **Equipment Safety:** Although citrate reduces scale by sequestering $\text{Ca}^{2+}$, introducing organic compounds into standing hot water tanks or steam boilers poses biological growth and thermal degradation risks if water is retained over long periods without regular flushing.

---

### Candidate Tooltip

Citrate is an organic buffer that chelates calcium and magnesium, reducing free mineral activity and suppressing scale. Added to water, it buffers brew pH differently than volatile bicarbonate. Distinct from native coffee acids, added citrate modifies extraction dynamics. High concentrations risk muted body or salty-sour off-notes.

===== SYNTHESIS INVESTIGATION =====
### Interaction framework

#### 1. Hardness, Alkalinity, and Extraction pH
Water chemistry in coffee extraction operates primarily through two independent axes: **General Hardness (GH)** and **Alkalinity (KH)**. 

*   **General Hardness (GH)** is defined as the total concentration of divalent cations, predominantly Calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$) and Magnesium ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$). These cations act as extracting agents that form coordination complexes with electron-rich organic species in the coffee matrix.
*   **Alkalinity (KH)** measures the solution's capacity to neutralize acids (acid-neutralizing capacity), governed almost entirely by the bicarbonate ($\text{HCO}_3^-$) / carbonate ($\text{CO}_3^{2-}$) equilibrium system. 
*   **Brewing pH** is dynamic. Ground coffee contains solubilizable organic acids (citric, malic, chlorogenic, quinic, phosphoric) that lower the slurry pH (typically from $6.0\text{--}8.0$ down to $4.8\text{--}5.2$). Water alkalinity acts as a buffer against this drop: higher alkalinity retains more neutralized carboxylate species ($\text{R-COO}^-$), decreasing perceived sensory acidity (titratable acidity), whereas low alkalinity allows native organic acids to remain protonated ($\text{R-COOH}$), increasing sensory sharpness.

#### 2. Ionic Strength and Activity Coefficients
The total ionic strength ($I$) of brewing water is defined as:
$$I = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i} c_i z_i^2$$
where $c_i$ is the molar concentration and $z_i$ is the charge of ion $i$. 

As ionic strength increases, the activity coefficients ($\gamma_i$) of dissolved species decrease according to the Debye-Hückel and Davies models:
$$a_i = \gamma_i c_i$$
This ionic atmosphere screen reduces the thermodynamic activity of ions and polar organic compounds in solution. Higher ionic strength enhances the solubility of moderately polar compounds through salting-in mechanisms at low-to-moderate mineral levels, while altering phase boundary kinetics between the water solvent and the hydrophobic lipid/emulsion phases of brewed coffee.

#### 3. Cation–Organic Ligand Complexation
Divalent cations ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{Ca}^{2+}$) act as Lewis acids, binding to electron-dense oxygen centers (hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl groups) present in chlorogenic acid isomers, caffeic acid, aliphatic acids, and oxygenated aromatic volatiles. 

*   **Magnesium ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$):** Ionic radius $\approx 72\text{ pm}$. Possesses a higher charge density than calcium. Density enables tighter binding energy to small, oxygen-rich organic ligands, enhancing the thermodynamic yield of polar, acid-associated flavor compounds.
*   **Calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$):** Ionic radius $\approx 100\text{ pm}$. Lower charge density yields slightly lower binding affinity per ion for light organic acids compared to $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, but its coordination geometry allows cross-linking with higher-molecular-weight polyphenols and carbohydrates, altering tactile body and viscosity.

Monovalent cations ($\text{Na}^+$, $\text{K}^+$) exhibit much lower complexation affinity for organic acids due to their low charge-to-radius ratio, functioning primarily via general ionic strength effects rather than targeted ligand extraction.

#### 4. Anion Effects
Anions modulate both extraction kinetics and sensory modulation:
*   **Bicarbonate ($\text{HCO}_3^-$):** Direct chemical sink for hydronium ions ($\text{H}^+$), shifting the equilibrium of extraction acids toward unprotonated, less sour forms.
*   **Chloride ($\text{Cl}^-$):** Non-buffering anion that alters solution polarizability. Enhances flavor impact and tactile sweetness at low-to-moderate concentrations through neural transduction mechanisms on the tongue, but suppresses volatile aromatics at high concentrations.
*   **Sulfate ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$):** Divalent anion that increases ionic strength rapidly ($z^2 = 4$). It modulates sensory dryness and perceived bitterness, interacting with hydrophobic coffee compounds to yield a crisp or astringent finish.
*   **Citrate ($\text{Citrate}^{3-}$):** Multidentate organic tri-anion ($z=-3$). Functions as both a strong buffer in the $\text{pH } 3.1\text{--}5.4$ range and a potent chelating agent for $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, competing with coffee ligands for divalent cations.

#### Direct Coffee Evidence
*   **Cation Extraction Efficiency:** Hendon et al. (2014) demonstrated via density functional theory (DFT) calculations and extraction trials that $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ preferentially extract target oxygen-rich flavor compounds (chlorogenic acid, citric acid) over monovalent systems, with $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ showing higher binding affinity per mass unit.
*   **Alkalinity and Titratable Acidity:** Navarini & Rivetti (2008) and Perez-Martinez et al. (2008) confirmed that water alkalinity directly dictates brewed espresso and filter coffee pH, showing a strong inverse correlation between water $\text{HCO}_3^-$ concentration and perceived sensory acidity.
*   **Volatile Headspace Suppression:** Research on coffee matrix interactions demonstrates that elevated mineral concentrations reduce headspace release of specific volatile organic compounds (VOCs) through salting-out and complexation phenomena in the liquid phase.

#### General Chemistry Inference
*   **Ionic Strength & Activity:** Standard Debye-Hückel aquatic chemistry dictates that increasing ionic strength reduces ion activity ($\gamma_i < 1$). Application of this principle to coffee extraction kinetics is thermodynamically sound but rarely measured in real-time coffee brewing literature.
*   **Sodium Bitterness Masking:** Neurophysiological research (e.g., Breslin & Beauchamp, 1995) proves $\text{Na}^+$ peripheral suppression of bitter taste receptors (TAS2Rs) at sub-threshold levels. Its application to coffee is a valid biochemical extrapolation, though modified by coffee's complex chlorogenic acid lactone matrix.

#### Coffee-Community Heuristics
*   **Sulfate-to-Chloride Ratio:** The concept that a specific $\text{SO}_4^{2-}:\text{Cl}^-$ ratio reliably shifts a coffee's profile between "crisp/dry" and "full/round" is an adaptation borrowed directly from brewing oceanography/beer chemistry (e.g., Burton-on-Trent water profiles). Controlled sensory trials in coffee matrices remain limited.
*   **Universal Mg:Ca Ratios (e.g., 3:1 or 2:1):** Often recommended in barista manuals as absolute targets. In practice, the ideal ratio shifts dynamically based on roast profile, bean origin, processing method, and total dissolved solids (TDS) target.

### Common attribution errors

A frequent error in water formulation for coffee is **single-ion attribution**—attributing a physical or sensory change to a single ion species while ignoring its counter-ion or the broader chemical equilibrium.

```
Incorrect Isolation:
  MgSO4 Addition ────> "Magnesium increases acidity and fruitiness."
  (Ignores the thermodynamic and sensory contribution of Sulfate)

Correct Systemic View:
  MgSO4 Addition ────> [ Mg2+ (Complexation + Hardness) ] 
                 └─> [ SO42- (Ionic Strength + Finish/Bitterness) ] 
                 └─> [ Δ Ionic Strength & Activity Coefficients ]
```

#### 1. The Salt Pairing Fallacy
Adding a salt introduces both a cation and an anion in stoichiometric equivalence:
*   Adding Magnesium via Magnesium Sulfate ($\text{MgSO}_4$) introduces $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ (extracting cation) and $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ (astringency/finish-modifying anion). Attributing a dry or bitter cup exclusively to "too much magnesium" fails to recognize that $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ alters taste perception at high levels.
*   Adding Bicarbonate via Sodium Bicarbonate ($\text{NaHCO}_3$) adds both buffering capacity ($\text{HCO}_3^-$) and sodium ($\text{Na}^+$). Reductions in cup sourness stem from $\text{HCO}_3^-$, whereas changes in perceived sweetness or bitterness suppression stem from $\text{Na}^+$.

#### 2. Charge Balance Requirement
Water formulations must maintain electroneutrality:
$$\sum (z_i \cdot [C_i]) = \sum (z_j \cdot [A_j])$$
You cannot adjust cations ($\text{Ca}^{2+}, \text{Mg}^{2+}, \text{Na}^+$) without shifting total anion concentrations ($\text{Cl}^-, \text{SO}_4^{2-}, \text{HCO}_3^-$). Every target profile represents a coupled chemical matrix.

#### 3. Confounding Hardness with Alkalinity
It is common to confuse high hardness with high alkalinity. A water system formulated with $150\text{ ppm CaCO}_3$ equivalent General Hardness (derived from $\text{CaCl}_2$ and $\text{MgSO}_4$) and $0\text{ ppm}$ Alkalinity will produce an intensely acidic, low-pH brew. Conversely, $20\text{ ppm}$ Hardness with $150\text{ ppm}$ Alkalinity will yield a flat, chalky, unbuffered-acid cup. Hardness dictates extraction potential; alkalinity dictates acidity suppression.

### Tooltip design rules

To ensure UI tooltips remain scientifically accurate without misleading the user or presenting subjective preferences as universal law, adhere to the following rules:

1.  **Strict 3-Part Structure:**
    *   **[Role]:** Identify the ion's primary chemical function in solution (e.g., divalent extraction cation, acid-neutralizing buffer, monovalent flavor modulator).
    *   **[Likely Cup Direction]:** State the probabilistic directional shift in sensory output (e.g., "Tends to enhance...", "Associated with increased...").
    *   **[Caveat]:** State the limit, trade-off, or confounder (e.g., anion pairing, scale formation, roast interaction).

2.  **Avoid Universal Taste Thresholds:**
    Never state fixed numbers like "At 30 ppm this tastes salty." Sensory thresholds vary significantly depending on total TDS, organic acid composition, roast level, and individual human olfactory/gustatory sensitivity.

3.  **Use Probabilistic Phrasing:**
    Replace absolute terms ("will make", "causes") with conditional language ("can shift", "may enhance", "typically highlights").

4.  **Acknowledge Matrix Dependencies:**
    Explicitly frame effects around roast depth or background water chemistry where applicable (e.g., light roasts expose acid shifts more prominently than dark roasts).

5.  **Strict Length Limit:**
    Tooltips must be concise for focus/hover states. **Maximum 55 words per row.**
